## Title

OEM Pressure Transmitter Qualification: A Procurement and Documentation Guide

## Subtitle

How industrial buyers, system integrators, importers, and distributors can define process fit, private-label scope, samples, and controlled documents before requesting a quotation.

## Topics

Industrial Automation, Pressure Sensors, Procurement, Manufacturing, Engineering

## Article Body

An OEM pressure-transmitter purchase is not simply a decision between two catalog models. The device has to fit the process, the machine interface, the documentation package, the labeling plan, and the approval path used by the buyer and end customer. If any of those boundaries are left open, a quotation can look complete while still being difficult to approve, import, install, or support.

For industrial buyers, system integrators, importers, and distributors, the practical question is not only, "Which pressure transmitter should I buy?" It is, "What must be true for this configuration to be accepted in our equipment program?" AccuMeasure's [OEM pressure-transmitter qualification guide](https://www.accumeasuretech.com/applications/pressure-transmitter-for-oem-equipment) starts from that wider requirement: range, overload conditions, wetted materials, process and electrical connections, output, enclosure, documentation, and the branding brief all need to be reviewed together.

## 1. Separate the Measurement Requirement From the OEM Scope

Start with the measurement job. State whether the pressure point is used for local indication, machine control, alarms, diagnostics, or a customer-facing display. Then specify the process medium, normal pressure, expected surges, operating temperature, connection, environmental conditions, and the performance requirement with its error basis. A pressure point that looks straightforward on a piping drawing can still require a different configuration when the normal pressure, transient conditions, or wetted-material requirement becomes clear.

Next, state the OEM scope independently. A standard product with a new logo is not the same project as a unit that needs a different label, packaging, documentation language, interface, enclosure, or design change. Treat those as separate review items in the RFQ. This avoids the common problem where the technical selection is discussed early but the commercial proposal assumes a standard product while the buyer expects a private-label program.

The published OEM application guidance refers to AM-PT300 pressure transmitters and AM-PG200 digital gauges as starting points for qualification, not as automatic answers for every project. A supplier should still confirm the quoted configuration against the actual process and program requirements in writing.

## 2. Make the Process and Interface Comparable

The first RFQ should let a supplier compare like with like. Include the pressure type, normal operating range, maximum or surge condition, medium, wetted-material needs, process connection, and mounting orientation. Add any vibration, washdown, outdoor exposure, temperature, or space constraint that could affect the enclosure or installation. The published pressure category guidance similarly starts with pressure type, normal and surge range, wetted materials, process connection, signal or display, power, enclosure, and area classification.

For the machine interface, explain how the pressure information will be used. Name the required signal or display, supply arrangement, connector or cable expectation, panel or field mounting constraints, and the controller or display that receives the value. If a system integrator will wire the instrument into a control cabinet, include the relevant electrical and documentation requirements before the quotation is finalized. Do not expect a supplier to infer an electrical interface from the word "OEM."

For distributors and importers, add the end market, required document language, the party responsible for technical approval, and any inspection checkpoint before shipment. That information helps separate a one-time sample review from a repeatable product program.

## 3. Treat Branding and Custom Work as Controlled Changes

Private labeling must be described precisely. Provide approved logo files, label text, serial-number rules, packaging content, document branding, and any language or artwork requirement. If the request extends beyond branding, identify the requested mechanical, electrical, firmware, or interface change separately. Each change creates a feasibility and validation question; it should not be folded into a generic instruction such as "customized pressure transmitter."

AccuMeasure's published customization workflow separates requirement review, feasibility checking, quotation and validation planning, engineering review, sample assembly and testing, customer evaluation, and production planning. That sequence is useful for procurement because it makes the handoffs visible. The buyer can define which drawings, samples, labels, documents, or approval records are needed at each stage rather than discovering a missing approval after production planning has begun.

Avoid sending protected artwork, customer drawings, or confidential specifications until the permitted use, ownership, and retention terms are agreed in writing. A useful RFQ can still define the scope, acceptance path, and open questions without attaching restricted files prematurely.

## 4. Ask for a Document Set That Matches the Quoted Configuration

A catalog page is a screening tool, not the final evidence for a purchase order. Before comparing quotations, request the current controlled documents that apply to the proposed model and configuration. The exact set depends on the project, but buyers commonly need the current datasheet, dimensional drawing, installation and wiring information, configuration or communication information where applicable, and the documents required by the customer or destination market.

When an inspection, test record, declaration, calibration record, or compliance document is needed, specify the exact document type and the point at which it will be reviewed. Ask the supplier to identify the document revision, scope, model identification, issuer where applicable, and how it relates to the quoted configuration. A statement that a document is "available" does not establish that it applies to the purchased item.

The same principle applies to any hazardous-area requirement. Provide the applicable classification and required protection concept in the RFQ, then request the current model-specific documentation for review. Do not infer coverage from a category name, a general brochure, or an image of a marking.

## 5. Define Sample Approval Before Commercial Handoff

For an OEM program, the sample is not just a small order. It is a qualification checkpoint. Agree what will be reviewed before the sample is built, what the buyer will inspect after receipt, what records must accompany it, and who has authority to approve or reject the configuration. List the acceptance criteria for process fit, installation, electrical interface, labels, packaging, documents, and any agreed inspection point.

Commercial details should be equally explicit. Request a quotation that identifies the proposed configuration, sample and production quantities, destination, packaging scope, delivery assumptions, and the commercial conditions tied to custom work. The official customization guidance notes that final project terms depend on the approved requirements. That is a reason to record open technical and commercial assumptions, not to rely on an informal verbal understanding.

For repeat purchasing, also agree how design, label, documentation, or supplier changes will be communicated and approved. A change-control rule protects both the buyer and supplier when an OEM program moves from an evaluated sample to a recurring order.

## Final Procurement Rule

Qualify the pressure transmitter and the OEM program together. Begin with the pressure duty and installation, then confirm the process and electrical interface, branding or custom boundary, document set, sample acceptance criteria, and commercial assumptions. An RFQ that says only "OEM pressure transmitter" leaves too much for a supplier to guess. A controlled qualification brief makes the quotation comparable and gives every party a written path from first review to production planning.
