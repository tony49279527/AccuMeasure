For industrial tanks, the choice between **capacitive** and **ultrasonic** level sensors comes down to your fluid properties, tank environment, and required accuracy. Here's a practical breakdown:

## Capacitive Level Sensors

**How they work:** Measure capacitance change between a probe and the tank wall (or a reference electrode) as fluid rises. The fluid acts as a dielectric.

**Best for:**
- Clean, non-conductive liquids (oils, solvents, chemicals)
- Small to medium tanks
- Point-level detection (high/low alarms) or continuous
- Hazardous areas (intrinsically safe versions available)
- High-pressure and high-temperature vessels (probe designs rated to 100+ bar, 200°C+)
- Sticky or coating fluids (rod/plate probes resist buildup better than some alternatives)

**Limitations:**
- Dielectric constant of the fluid must be stable (temperature, moisture, and composition changes affect reading)
- Not ideal for conductive fluids without insulated probes
- Buildup on the probe drifts calibration
- Foam, vapors, and condensation can cause false readings

**Typical accuracy:** ±1–2% of span (continuous), reliable point detection

## Ultrasonic Level Sensors

**How they work:** Transmit sound pulses downward, measure time-of-flight to the liquid surface.

**Best for:**
- Large tanks, silos, sumps, reservoirs
- Wastewater, slurries, viscous or conductive liquids
- Non-contact measurement (no contamination, no cleaning of probe)
- Open-channel flow and wide level ranges
- Corrosive fluids (with appropriate housing material)

**Limitations:**
- Foam, vapor, dust, and condensation attenuate the signal
- Requires headroom (dead zone ~0.25–0.5 m typical)
- Temperature stratification affects sound velocity (many units have temperature compensation)
- Agitated surfaces or tank internals (ladders, pipes) cause false echoes
- Not suitable for vacuum or pressurized tanks (unless special models)
- Blocked by obstructions near the transducer

**Typical accuracy:** ±0.25% of span (good units), ±1% typical industrial

## Quick Selection Guide

| Condition | Better Choice |
|---|---|
| Clean oil/solvent, small tank | Capacitive |
| Wastewater, slurry, dirty fluid | Ultrasonic |
| High pressure (>10 bar) | Capacitive |
| Vacuum tank | Capacitive |
| Foamy surface | Capacitive (or guided wave radar) |
| Hygienic/food (CIP) | Capacitive (flush probe) |
| Large tank, long range | Ultrasonic |
| Corrosive liquid, non-contact needed | Ultrasonic |
| Sticky/coating fluid | Ultrasonic (non-contact) |
| Hazardous area, simple point level | Capacitive |

## Practical Notes

- **If your fluid is conductive and clean**, consider **guided wave radar (GWR)** — it beats both capacitive and ultrasonic in most industrial tank applications and is largely immune to foam, vapor, and dielectric changes.
- **If you need point-level only**, capacitive or vibratory (tuning fork) switches are usually cheaper and more reliable than ultrasonics.
- **Dielectric drift** is the #1 field problem with capacitive sensors — always verify fluid composition is stable, or choose a sensor with auto-calibration.
- **Ultrasonics fail silently** in foam or heavy vapor — if you can't tolerate missed readings, GWR or radar is safer.

**Rule of thumb:** Clean, stable fluid in a small or pressurized tank → capacitive. Dirty, variable, or large-volume application in an open tank → ultrasonic. When in doubt, guided wave radar is often the best compromise.
