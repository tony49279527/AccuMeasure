In water treatment, both radar and ultrasonic level sensors are widely used, but they have distinctly different strengths. Here's a practical comparison focused on water/wastewater applications.

## Quick Summary

| Factor | Radar | Ultrasonic |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | Electromagnetic (microwave) pulses | Acoustic (sound) pulses |
| Measurement | Time-of-flight of EM wave | Time-of-flight of sound |
| Best for | Harsh, vaporous, foamy, condensing | Clean, calm, open tanks |
| Cost | Higher (2–5×) | Lower |
| Accuracy | ±2–5 mm typical | ±0.25–1% of range |
| Range | Up to 30–80 m | Up to 10–15 m typically |

## How They Differ Physically

**Radar** transmits microwave pulses (typically 6, 26, or 80 GHz) and measures the return echo. EM waves are largely unaffected by air temperature, pressure, humidity, vapor, and dust.

**Ultrasonic** transmits acoustic pulses (typically 20–200 kHz) and measures the echo. Sound speed varies with temperature (~0.17%/°C) and is degraded by vapor, foam, wind, and stratification.

## Where Each Excels in Water Treatment

### Radar wins on:
- **Vapor and steam** — headworks, hot process tanks, digesters
- **Foam layers** — aeration basins, some DAF units (80 GHz handles foam better; it can even track foam surface + true liquid level in some models)
- **Condensation** — covered tanks where ultrasonic transducers "blind" themselves
- **Long range** — large clarifiers, storage reservoirs, wet wells 15 m+
- **Aggressive atmospheres** — H₂S in sewers, chemical dosing tanks (with proper antenna material)
- **Vacuum/pressure** — sealed or pressurized tanks
- **No beam spreading issues** — narrow beam (especially 80 GHz) works in tight or obstructed spaces

### Ultrasonic wins on:
- **Clean water, open tanks** — clearwells, backwash tanks, chemical storage with calm surfaces
- **Short range, high resolution** — smaller tanks and sumps
- **Budget** — often 30–60% cheaper per point
- **Simple, non-contact setup** — easy to commission, easy to troubleshoot
- **Non-metallic tanks** — no interference concerns at low frequencies

## Practical Application Guide

| Application | Recommended |
|---|---|
| Raw sewage wet well | **Radar** (foam, grease, H₂S) |
| Aeration basin | **Radar** (foam) |
| Clarifier (open) | Either; radar if foaming |
| Digester / sludge | **Radar** |
| Chemical day tank (calm) | Ultrasonic OK, radar preferred |
| Clearwell / storage | Either |
| Backwash / filter tank | Ultrasonic OK |
| Hot process water | **Radar** |
| Covered tank with condensation | **Radar** |
| Small sump, tight budget | Ultrasonic |

## Key Installation Considerations

- **Ultrasonic**: needs temperature compensation; avoid direct sunlight on transducer; keep beam clear of walls, ladders, mixers; 0.3–0.5 m minimum blanking distance.
- **Radar**: 80 GHz has very narrow beam (great for nozzles/standpipes); 26 GHz is a workhorse; 6 GHz for very long range or heavy foam. Horn antenna fouling is a real issue in sludge/grease — consider flush or lens antennas.
- Both: mount away from fill points and turbulence; use stilling wells only if necessary (radar generally doesn't need one).

## Cost Reality

- Ultrasonic: ~$400–1,500 per point
- Radar (26 GHz): ~$1,000–2,500
- Radar (80 GHz): ~$1,500–3,500

When you factor in reliability, reduced maintenance, and fewer call-outs, radar often wins TCO even in wastewater where ultrasonic was historically standard.

## Bottom Line

- **Ultrasonic** for clean, calm, short-range, budget-sensitive measurements.
- **Radar** for anything with foam, vapor, condensation, long range, or where reliability matters more than initial cost.

In modern water treatment plants, radar (especially 80 GHz) has largely become the default for critical level measurements, with ultrasonic retained for straightforward clean-water applications.
