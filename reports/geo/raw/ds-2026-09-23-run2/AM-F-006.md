# AM-F-006 — faq / level

Query: can ultrasonic level sensors measure chemical tanks

Engine: deepseek-chat (no web browsing) (model: deepseek-flash)

---

**Yes, ultrasonic level sensors can measure chemical tanks**, but whether they are the right choice depends heavily on the specific chemical, tank conditions, and process requirements. Here's a practical breakdown:

## Why They Work Well in Many Chemical Tanks
- **Non-contact measurement** – the sensor never touches the chemical, so it avoids corrosion, contamination, and material compatibility issues.
- **Easy retrofit** – mounts on top of the tank, no need to cut into the side or drain the tank.
- **Works with most liquids** – acids, bases, solvents, water-based solutions, slurries (with caveats).
- **Cost-effective** – generally cheaper than radar or guided-wave for basic applications.

## Key Limitations to Watch For

**1. Vapors and foam**
- Heavy fumes, steam, or condensation can absorb or scatter the ultrasonic pulse, causing false or lost readings.
- Foaming chemicals (soaps, surfactants, some acids) can absorb the signal entirely.

**2. Temperature and pressure**
- Most ultrasonic sensors are limited to roughly **-40 °C to +80 °C** and near-atmospheric pressure.
- Pressurized or high-temperature chemical reactors usually need radar or guided-wave instead.

**3. Chemical compatibility of the sensor housing**
- Even non-contact sensors have a transducer face (often PVDF, Teflon, or Kynar) exposed to vapors. Aggressive vapors (e.g., HF, concentrated chlorine) can attack it.
- Always check the manufacturer's chemical compatibility chart.

**4. Tank geometry**
- Narrow tanks, baffles, agitators, or ladders create false echoes.
- Very tall tanks (>15 m) may exceed the sensor's range.

**5. Changing dielectric / density**
- Ultrasonic measures **time-of-flight** to the surface, so it's largely independent of density and dielectric constant — this is actually an advantage over capacitance or radar in some cases.

**6. Condensation on the transducer**
- Common in chilled tanks or humid environments; heated transducers or purged models help.

## When to Choose Something Else
| Condition | Better Alternative |
|---|---|
| Heavy foam or vapor | Guided-wave radar (TDR) |
| High pressure/temperature | Radar or differential pressure |
| Very aggressive vapors | Radar with PTFE antenna |
| Hygienic / food-grade chemicals | Sanitary ultrasonic or radar |
| Sticky or coating chemicals | Non-contact radar |

## Practical Tips for Chemical Tanks
- Use **PVDF or PTFE-faced transducers** for corrosive service.
- Add a **stillwell** or standpipe to isolate from foam/turbulence.
- Mount away from fill streams, agitators, and tank walls (typically ≥ 300 mm from walls).
- For hazardous areas, specify **intrinsically safe (Ex ia) or explosion-proof** models.
- Consider **temperature compensation** and, in some cases, a **reference target** to auto-correct for vapor speed changes.

**Bottom line:** Ultrasonic sensors are widely used in chemical tank level measurement — especially for aqueous solutions, mild acids/bases, and non-foaming, near-atmospheric tanks. For harsh vapors, foam, pressure, or high temperature, radar (especially guided-wave) is usually the safer bet.
